#

Addressed feedback rate (AFR)

One of the primary reasons a complex open source project like Chromium relies on code review is as a tool for learning, to help newer contributors learn the engineering practices of the community. When the project is healthy, contributors learn from their reviewers, reducing the need for their review and ultimately becoming reviewers themselves to help teach the next generation of contributors.

Addressed feedback rate (AFR): addressed review comments per 100 lines written. That is the comment threads started that led to a change in the code, for every 100 new or changed lines, over the changes someone landed. Removed lines don't count, since they draw almost no feedback. Large-scale changes and other large mechanical changes are left out.

      #

      Feedback index

      Feedback index: a contributor's addressed comments over the number their changes would be expected to draw: everyone else's AFR at the same experience, times how much feedback the changes' approvals usually draw on everyone else's changes (their reviewer factor: 1.00× for the typical reviewer). Comparing by reviewer predicts feedback better than comparing by directory, and it means picking a lenient reviewer doesn't lower anyone's index (see the methodology below). A change's reviewers are those who approved it, not those who commented on it: a commenter may not have approved it, and a reviewer may not have commented. 1.00× is typical (the median contributor); below 1 means less feedback than expected, above 1 more. Because it compares each change with others at the same experience, the index doesn't fall as someone gains experience the way the rate does: it shows how someone's feedback compares at every stage.

        Methodology and details

        Why compare by reviewer, not directory

        What each comparison predicts

        Reviewers within a directory

        Why approvers, not commenters

        The reviewers are the people who approved the change (its Reviewed-by lines), not the people who commented on it. Who commented is part of the outcome being measured: only about half of changes draw a reviewer comment at all, and a change nobody commented on has no addressed feedback by definition. Comparing by commenters would seem to predict much better, but only because it already knows the answer. It would expect almost nothing of a change that needed no fixes, taking away the credit that change earns, and expect more of a change because it drew many comments, cancelling part of the feedback it should count. The attention set has the same problem, since commenting puts a reviewer back in it. Nearly every change (96%) has an approver, whether or not they commented. The cost is that a reviewer who commented without approving doesn't count toward the comparison.

          By organization

          The median contributor's AFR over their changes whose main directory (two levels) is in each directory, and over their changes from each organization, for the directories and organizations with the most contributors ( or more changes each). Directories matter much less than experience or size, except that imported third-party code and configuration directories (such as third_party/libvpx/ or testing/buildbot/) draw almost no feedback per line. Organizations differ, partly through their contributors' experience and the directories they work in. The feedback index compares by reviewer, which carries most of what the directory says (see above), and not by organization.

          Nothing in the data indicates that contributors outside Google get tougher reviews than Googlers do; if anything, the opposite. The median index of contributors outside Google is somewhat lower than Googlers', about half of that gap comes from the directories they work in, and reviewers at Google leave as much addressed feedback on colleagues' changes as on outsiders'. Comparing within an organization would not predict feedback any better, so the index doesn't (details).

          Working across many directories doesn't raise anyone's index much. A change in a directory new to its author draws about 1.2× the expected feedback, fading over their first 16 or so commits there, but large changes into new directories draw much less than expected and cancel most of it. People who do much of their work in directories new to them have about the same index as everyone else. Correcting for it would move almost nobody and predict feedback barely better, so the index doesn't (details).

          How it's measured

          Changes are the merged, human-owned Chromium changes on main that landed from to , leaving out mechanical ones (100 or more files, or mostly repeated lines) and large-scale changes (CLs whose commit message cites Chromium's LSC process, or that git cl split uploaded), as on the Review Balance page. A review comment is a comment thread on Gerrit started by someone other than the change's owner (its commenter), bots left out, on a file or the commit message (general comments on the whole change aren't counted: they can't be tied to a directory, and few lead to a change in the code). It is addressed when the owner's reply led to a change in the code, as judged by a regular expression for “Done”-style replies and by an AI model (Claude Haiku) for the rest (how). Lines are the new or changed lines (Gerrit's insertions); removed lines don't count, because they draw almost no feedback, and changes that only remove lines are left out.

          An AFR is pooled: all the addressed comments on a set of changes per 100 of all their lines. Experience is the number of CLs the owner had landed on main, counting the change itself (their commits since 2008, reverts left out), in bins that double: 8 or fewer, 9–16, 17–32 and so on up to 4k and more. A contributor's rate in a bin counts with or more changes there.

          The feedback index divides the addressed comments on your changes by the number they would be expected to draw: each change's lines times everyone else's AFR at the change's experience (pooled: all their addressed comments per 100 of all their lines), times its reviewer factor. A change's reviewers are its approvers (the Reviewed-by lines of the landed commit, those who voted Code-Review), and its factor is their mean. Each reviewer's factor is fitted together with a factor for every author: a change is expected to draw experience × author × reviewer, each factor is what it must be given the others, and each is pulled toward 1.00× until it has enough changes behind it. So a reviewer isn't counted as strict because they mostly approve newcomers. Your own changes are left out of what yours are compared with: the AFR at each experience is everyone else's, and each of your reviewers' factors is refitted without your changes (keeping every author's factor as fitted), so a reviewer who approves few changes but yours counts as typical rather than taking on your feedback. The table of your reviewers shows these factors, so they can differ a little from what others see for the same reviewer. The factors cover every year (see “Drift over time” above). Pooled rates are lower than the typical contributor's (big changes draw less feedback per line), so the expected comments are scaled to make the median contributor's index 1.00×. A directory's index in the tables is its addressed comments over the expected ones on the lines there, so it adds up to the overall index. The spread of everyone's index (the tiles and the over-time chart) counts contributors with or more changes in the period.

          Addresses are one person only on evidence: a Gerrit owner and the git author of the same changes, or a reviewer's address and the account that cast the vote. Sharing a name across chromium.org and google.com isn't enough. The data has one file per person, named by their address in storage that can't be listed, so this page can only find someone whose address you already know. Everything comes from public Gerrit and git data. Code and method: docs/review-feedback.md.

          Reviewer: someone who approved the change: named in the landed commit's Reviewed-by lines (they voted Code-Review), whether or not they commented. The index compares by reviewer.
