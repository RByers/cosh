Chromium Open Source Health - Review Balance

Draft: under development

This page is an early draft. Its analysis has been reviewed once but is still very messy, overly complex and with some AI slop. It will be simplified and clarified to the key takeaways and so is probably not worth reading in detail yet.

The question

Chromium has grown for two decades through code review: experts give feedback, learn to need less of it, and in time they review and own code themselves and train the next . AI tools now make changes cheaper to write than to review. This page asks whether that cycle still holds, and how the community can adapt to agentic engineering in a way that improves product quality, robustness, engineering rigour and velocity, and keeps growing its people into expert engineers.

The signals

  • Balance: how much review feedback each CL gets for its size and directory (the feedback index), and how many CLs each reviewer carries.
  • Speed: hours from being sent for review to the first substantive review, and review time: the weekday hours a CL spent waiting on a reviewer in Gerrit's attention set.
  • Quality: how much a CL changes after review, review rounds, reverts within 30 days, −1 votes and abandoned CLs.
  • Growth: whether people need less review feedback as they gain tenure and know an area, and whether they grow in velocity, breadth or reviewing, compared with chance and across cohorts.
  • Carrying the project: moving from author to reviewer to OWNER, who reviews , and how many mentees become mentors.

What we see so far

Two growth trajectories are the clearest patterns in the data, and the ones to protect. The review feedback a CL draws for its size and directory falls steadily with the author's tenure, and the share of people's work that is reviewing rises steadily with years since joining, on the same path for every join year since 2017. Most people follow both. Among people still landing CLs steadily, about one in five is well behind on each in a given year. Behind on feedback, that rate has held level since 2023; behind on reviewing, it has risen from about one in six in 2024 to one in four in 2026 (details).

Per-author velocity has crept up since 2023 as the pool of active authors shrank, and in 2026 CLs got bigger too. Review feedback per line fell: like for like, a 2026 CL gets about a third less than one in 2023. Waits shortened in 2025 and are back to 2023 levels, and reverts haven't risen. People who joined in 2025 land more CLs and review others sooner than earlier joiners, and draw less review feedback. Fewer people review than write CLs, and each reviewer now sees half again as many CLs as in 2023. Fewer mid-career reviewers approve ' CLs, and fewer go on to mentor others or move into reviewing, trends that began before AI tools.

Following the same people separates two explanations for the drop in review feedback. Established authors get fewer threads but their CLs are no better on what reviewers don't control, so reviewers seem to be easing off a little. who joined in 2025 do better than matched 2024 joiners even on those measures, so they seem to arrive better equipped, such as with effective AI tools. More CLs are abandoned in 2026, at every tenure. Security fixes jumped more than tenfold in 2026, a new load on reviewers. No single signal here separates AI from other changes in the project.

What may be driving it

Reading it together, in order of how much the data back each one:

  • More CLs for the same reviewers. This is the one firm driver. CLs rose by a third from 2024 to 2026 Q3 and by three quarters in October year on year, the median CL is half again as big, newcomers' share of CLs tripled (Where review is going), and the reviewers themselves land a fifth more CLs of their own. The reviewer pool is the same size as in 2024, so every one of those CLs lands on the same people, who answer later and say less per CL. AI-marked CLs are too few to say how much of the new volume is AI-written (AI-marked CLs).
  • A reviewer pipeline that thinned before AI tools. New regular reviewers fell to – in 2025, the fewest since 2017, and the share of approvals given by people under 3 years on the project fell in 21 of 22 big directories (The training chain). The 2026 surge found no new reviewers to absorb it. Platform directories, which attract and promote the fewest new people, had the least slack.
  • Attention follows the volume. Platform reviewers didn't stop reviewing their own directories; they added reviewing in chrome/browser, where most of the new CLs are, and more CLs of their own. Each blink or content/browser CL still gets as many reviewers as before, but every one of those reviewers has a longer personal queue. This is a reading of the figures above, not a measured cause.
  • Not drivers. The kinds of CL being sent (about a quarter of the rise, all of it size); the shift to longer-serving approvers in blink (none of it, every tenure band slowed alike); and load per reviewer within a directory, which rose most where waits rose least.
  • What these data can't see. Time spent outside review (design, incidents, meetings, planning), working hours and time zones beyond the UTC hour, how much of the new code is AI-written, and whether authors now send CLs to fewer, busier reviewers by choice or because OWNERS files leave no one else.

Arriving better equipped, or easing off?

Review per CL fell in 2026. Either authors arrive better equipped and need less, or reviewers ask less of the same work. Following the same people from 2024 to 2026, on like-for-like CLs (same size, directory and author tenure), separates the two: if authors arrived better equipped, the measures reviewers don't control would improve too.

The same – reviewers started – fewer threads per review. The same – authors received – fewer threads, but on the measures reviewers don't control they were no better: their CLs changed as much after review and were reverted as often, and the share abandoned rose by –. They did upload more versions before sending a CL for review, which fits self-revision with AI tools, but it hasn't shown up as less change after review. For established authors, then, the evidence leans modestly to reviewers easing off. Since the threads that remain drive as much change as before, reviewers may be dropping low-value comments rather than real problems.

Adoption by directory (speculative)

Agent instruction files (AGENTS.md, GEMINI.md, .claude/ and the like) don't predict AI use. Only 15 of the 115 busiest directories have one, only 5 of those ever reach a visible share of marked CLs, and 36 reach it without a file. One enthusiast can add a file nobody else uses. A better treatment is the first quarter in which at least 5% of a directory's CLs are marked. In the 41 directories that reached it, review feedback per CL drifted down afterwards ( about 0.1 lower, with flat trends before), the direction of thinner review once AI is in use. With so few directories, no quarter's change is distinguishable from zero. Most AI use is unmarked, so every comparison here is biased toward finding nothing.

Other commit footers that might signal shortcuts, such as Owners-Override, Auto-Submit and No-Try, have been flat since 2021 and mark small, routine CLs, so they say little about the review balance.

Why mechanical CLs are left out

A CL counts as mechanical with 100 or more files, or with 10 or more files of which at least 80% of changed lines are repeats (the same lines repeated across files). The rule doesn't look at review counts, so it can't bias the review measures it filters.

Past about 50 files, review feedback per file collapses: CLs with 100 to 199 files got – threads per file, against – for 10 to 19 files. Repetitive diffs behave the same at any size.

#

Defined: the feedback index

Review feedback is counted in reviewer threads: comment threads on a CL started by someone other than its author (bots left out). A vote or a lone "LGTM" starts none. Bigger CLs draw more of them, and some directories review more closely than others. So each CL is compared with a norm: the mean threads, over 2023–24, of CLs of the same size and the same main 2-level directory (such as chrome/browser). Size uses ten buckets that each double the last: under 4 lines, 4–7, 8–15 and so on up to 1,024 or more. Where a directory had fewer than 30 CLs of a size, the norm is the mean for that size alone. A group's feedback index is its CLs' threads added up, divided by their norms added up:

feedback index = Σ reviewer threads ÷ Σ 2023–24 norms

1.0 means the group got as much review feedback as like-for-like CLs did in 2023–24; 0.67 means a third less; 2.0, twice as much. Because size and directory are held fixed, CLs getting bigger or work moving between areas doesn't move the index; only the feedback per like CL does.

In 2023–24 the smallest tenth of CLs, up to – changed lines, drew – threads on average, and – of them none. The largest tenth, over – lines, drew –. Threads per CL are lumpy, so the index is meant for groups (a quarter, a tenure band, a cohort), not single CLs: a single CL's index says little.

Defined: newcomers and tenure

Tenure is counted from a person's first commit on chromium/src main, under any of their email addresses. A newcomer is anyone in their first year after that commit. Each CL is placed by its author's tenure on the day it closed, so the same person writes newcomer CLs for twelve months and then stops. Experienced engineers moving in from other projects count as newcomers too: the page measures time in Chromium, not experience. A person "joined" in the year of that first commit, which is how the cohorts are named.

In 2026 newcomers' CLs were – of all approvals on main, against – in 2023. "Newcomer approvals" are approvals (Reviewed-by) of those CLs, grouped by the approver's own tenure.

#

How it's measured

Data
Chromium's public Gerrit review data (comments, threads and NoteDb history with uploads and the attention set) from July 2023, every reviewed CL's patch sets, and chromium/src main's full git history, including Reviewed-by footers from mid-2017 and OWNERS files from 2012.
People
One person across addresses: the same username at chromium.org and google.com, old SVN-era addresses, and the Gerrit owner and git author of the same CL. Tenure counts from a person's first commit on main. Bots and service accounts are left out. No individual is named or ranked.
Substantive review
An inline reviewer comment, or a reviewer thread the author answered. A lone "LGTM" is not substantive review.
Feedback index
Reviewer threads ÷ the 2023–24 mean for CLs of the same size (ten log-spaced buckets) and main 2-level directory (defined above).
Newcomer
An author in their first year after their first commit on main (defined above).
Lines changed after review
The added and removed lines of the first reviewed patch set's diff against the final one's, compared as multisets, so a rebase changes nothing.
Growth
Per person, the least-squares slope of each path over the half-years of a two-year window (three or more measured), shrunk toward zero by its noise and compared with slopes from shuffled half-years. Individual slopes are never published.
AI attribution
Most AI use leaves no marker, so the before/after comparison can't separate AI from other changes in 2025–26: team growth, new hires, reorganisations, or tooling unrelated to AI.
Left out
Abandoned and open CLs; release branches; other repositories (V8, Skia, DevTools). Gerrit history before July 2023 isn't fetched, so pre-AI review measures cover only 18 months. 2026 Q4 is incomplete and is left out of the quarterly charts.
